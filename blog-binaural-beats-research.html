Binaural beats and “healing frequencies”: what the research actually shows

A meta-analysis finds a medium effect in lab studies. An EEG review finds the usual explanation unproven. And the famous “healing” pitches rest on almost nothing. Here is how to tell them apart.

Search for “focus music” or “sleep sounds” and you will find hours of audio built on rhythmic tones: binaural beats, isochronic tones and “healing frequencies.” The claims around them range from modest to magical. The research is more interesting than either extreme, and a good deal thinner than the marketing suggests.

Three different things that get called “frequencies”

Binaural beats. Each ear hears a steady tone, and the two tones differ slightly in pitch. The brain hears a third, pulsing tone at the difference between them. It only works on headphones, because each ear has to get its own tone.

Isochronic tones and tremolo. A single tone switched on and off, or swelled and softened, at a steady rate. The pulse is in the sound itself, so speakers work too.

“Solfeggio” or “healing” frequencies. A specific pitch, such as 528 Hz or 777 Hz. This is a note, not a beat: nothing pulses. The idea behind it is different from the first two, and so is the evidence.

What the best evidence on binaural beats shows

The most-cited summary is a meta-analysis of 22 studies (35 effect sizes) on memory, attention, anxiety measures and pain perception. Across them, binaural beats had a medium-sized effect (g = 0.45). Listening before a task, or before and during it, did better than listening only during it, and longer exposure tended to do better than shorter.1

That is a real signal, with limits worth stating. These were mostly small lab studies with short tasks, and they used different beat rates, carriers and listening times. A pooled effect in the lab says little about a particular track in everyday use.

Does the brain actually “follow” the beat? The usual explanation is entrainment: brain waves are said to shift toward the beat rate. A systematic review of 14 EEG studies found 5 consistent with that, 8 contradicting it and 1 mixed, with methods too varied to compare cleanly.2 So the mechanism most often quoted is the least settled part of the story.

Isochronic tones: far less tested

Almost all of the controlled research uses binaural beats; every study in the meta-analysis above did.1 Isochronic tones and tremolo are often described as working the same way, but that is an assumption carried over, not a finding.

“Healing frequencies”: very little to go on

The study most often cited for 528 Hz had 9 participants, who heard the same piano music tuned to 528 Hz and to the standard 440 Hz on separate days.3 That is a pilot, not proof. For 777 Hz we found no controlled study at all. It is fair to treat a particular pitch as a listening preference, and unfair to sell it as more than that.

How to read the claims

Rhythmic-tone audio is a reasonable thing to try as background sound for focus or winding down. The honest framing is “some lab studies suggest small-to-moderate effects, and the mechanism is unproven,” not “this will change your brain.” Be wary of any track that promises a specific outcome or a timeline, or that pairs a frequency with a named condition.

A few practical points if you listen: binaural beats need headphones; keep the volume comfortable; don’t listen while driving or doing anything that needs your full attention; and if you have epilepsy or a seizure disorder, check with a doctor first.
