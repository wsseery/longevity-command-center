What a DNA test can and can't tell you about food and inflammation

Some of the ways a body handles food are written in its genes: how fast it clears histamine, whether it keeps making lactase, whether it carries the risk genes for celiac disease. That makes a genetic test sound like the shortcut to an answer. For most food reactions it isn't — but it is better at ruling things out than most people realise.

A lot of people live with symptoms that never quite become a diagnosis. Flushing or itching after certain meals. Bloating, loose stools, headaches, a stuffy nose that comes and goes with what was on the plate. Routine bloodwork comes back unremarkable, and the working conclusion becomes “something I eat doesn't agree with me.”

It matters in longevity terms because chronic inflammation is one of the hallmarks of aging: it was added to the framework in its 2023 update, “Hallmarks of aging: an expanding universe.” A food that keeps provoking your immune and digestive systems is not a neutral background detail. And several of the mechanisms behind food reactions do have a genetic side. Here is what the evidence says each one can and cannot tell you.

Histamine: the enzyme genes are common, and that's the problem

Histamine is in food — aged, cured, fermented and leftover foods especially — and the body clears it with two enzymes: diamine oxidase (DAO), mostly in the gut, and histamine-N-methyltransferase (HNMT). When intake outruns breakdown, histamine builds up. That is the working model of histamine intolerance.

DAO is made from the AOC1 gene, and four common variants in it (rs10156191, rs1049742, rs1049793 and rs2052129) have been linked to lower DAO activity. They show up on many consumer DNA reports, so it is tempting to read one as a diagnosis. The data say not to:

  • The variants are everywhere. An earlier study (Maintz et al., 2011) found three of the four at frequencies of roughly 40% to 50%.
  • Carrying one barely separates patients from healthy people. In a 2024 pilot study of 100 people with histamine-intolerance symptoms and 100 controls, 79% of patients and 72% of controls carried at least one variant — a difference that was not statistically significant.
  • Two copies may matter more than one. The same study found homozygous risk genotypes in 36% of patients versus 9% of controls, with the clearest gap at rs2052129 TT (19% versus 0%). It was a small pilot, and its authors concluded that the presence of these variants “does not seem to be enough as a suitable diagnostic biomarker.”

The Swiss Interest Group Histamine Intolerance (SIGHI), whose food list underpins our histamine scores, puts it more bluntly: no laboratory test can diagnose histamine intolerance on its own. In SIGHI's words, “the only reliable method to diagnose HIT is an elimination diet lasting several weeks,” followed by reintroducing foods one at a time to find an individual threshold. That is why SIGHI's food compatibility list is the useful tool here, not a gene report.

The tryptase gene: a genetic finding that is easy to miss

One genetic trait does connect directly to mast-cell symptoms. In 2016, a team at the U.S. National Institutes of Health reported in Nature Genetics that elevated baseline blood tryptase — found in an estimated 4–6% of the general population — traced to extra copies of the TPSAB1 gene, which encodes alpha-tryptase. Families carrying the extra copies reported flushing and itching, irritable-bowel-type symptoms, chronic pain, joint hypermobility and dysautonomia, and people with three copies had higher tryptase and more symptoms than people with two. The condition is now called hereditary alpha-tryptasemia.

Two things about it are worth knowing. It was found by counting gene copies, which is a different measurement from the single-letter variants most consumer DNA reports list. And the first clue is an ordinary blood tryptase level, which is a question to raise with a physician, not something to infer from a food log.

Other inherited digestion traits

TraitGenetic sideWhat a gene result can do
LactoseA regulatory element near LCT (inside the neighbouring MCM6 gene) decides whether lactase production continues into adulthoodExplain a pattern. About 65% of people have a reduced ability to digest lactose after infancy, so not making lactase is the global norm, not a defect
Celiac diseaseHLA-DQ2 / DQ8Mostly rule it out. 30–40% of people carry these genes, and only about 3% of carriers develop celiac disease, but testing negative for both puts its negative predictive value above 99%
Sucrose and starchSI (sucrase-isomaltase)Flag a lead, not settle one. Rare loss-of-function mutations cause congenital deficiency, and a common variant (p.Val15Phe) cut sucrase activity by about 35% in the lab and was associated with irritable bowel syndrome

Celiac disease is the clearest case of genetics ruling something out rather than in. Its global prevalence is about 1.4% by blood antibodies and 0.7% by biopsy, far below the 30–40% who carry the risk genes. One practical point from the U.S. National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases: doctors don't recommend starting a gluten-free diet before diagnostic testing, because it can affect the results.

Before you trust a raw-data report

Much of the genetic information people bring to this question comes from downloading their consumer-test raw data into a third-party interpreter. That data was not built for clinical use. In a 2018 study in Genetics in Medicine, a clinical lab re-tested 49 patients' samples and found that about 40% of the variants reported in their raw data were not confirmed. Any result you intend to act on deserves confirmation by a clinical-grade test and interpretation by a professional.

Lectins: no gene to test, and thinner evidence

Lectins are the other half of our food-intolerance toolkit, and they sit on much weaker ground. There is no known gene test for lectin sensitivity. What is measured is the food side: a 1980 survey of 88 commonly eaten foods found lectin activity in 29 of them, about a third. That survey also noted that dry heat does not always destroy that activity.

The European Food Safety Authority reviewed plant lectins in January 2026. Its firm conclusion is narrow: inadequately processed beans, whose main lectin is phytohaemagglutinin, pose a real risk of acute illness, and proper soaking and boiling prevent it. On long-term effects in people, it found human data “very sparse” and called for more study. The popular lectin-avoidance food lists, led by Dr. Steven Gundry's The Plant Paradox (2017), go much further than that evidence.

How we score lectins — and the caveat that goes with every score. Our lectin ratings start from the Plant Paradox lists and are cross-checked against measured lectin-content literature. They are the weakest-evidenced of the three scales in the toolkit, and the tool says so wherever it shows one. Read a lectin score as a hypothesis to test on yourself, not a finding.

What to do with this

  • Read genetics as context, not a verdict. Gene results for celiac risk can usefully rule a condition out, and a lactase result can explain a pattern. A DAO variant on its own explains very little, because most people without symptoms carry one too.
  • Keep a plain symptom log. What you ate, when, and how you felt some hours later. It is the one input that is about you rather than a population.
  • Separate the mechanisms. A reaction to aged cheese and leftovers, a reaction to milk, and a reaction to wheat point in different directions. A single “inflammatory foods” list hides which one you are dealing with.
  • Bring it to a clinician before removing food groups — a physician or a registered dietitian — especially before cutting gluten (testing comes first) or the legumes and vegetables the longevity literature mostly recommends.
