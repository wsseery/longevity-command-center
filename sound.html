Sound, tones and beats: a plain guide

Binaural beats, isochronic tones, tremolo and “healing frequencies” are four different things with very different evidence. Here is how to tell them apart.

Rhythmic-tone audio (binaural beats, isochronic tones, “healing frequencies”) is one of the most popular kinds of background sound for focus, rest and sleep. This page sets out what each kind is, what the research does and doesn’t support, and how to listen sensibly. It is a reference, not a recommendation.

What’s what

Binaural beats. Each ear hears a steady tone, and the two differ slightly in pitch. You hear a slow pulse at the difference between them. Headphones are required: each ear has to receive its own tone.

Isochronic tones. One tone, switched on and off at a steady rate. The pulse is in the sound itself, so speakers work.

Tremolo (amplitude modulation). One tone that swells and softens at a steady rate. It is gentler than an isochronic pulse and also works on speakers.

“Solfeggio” or “healing” frequencies. A specific pitch, such as 528 Hz or 777 Hz. Nothing pulses: it is a note, chosen for its claimed meaning rather than for a rhythm.

Carrier and beat rate. Track descriptions often give two numbers. The carrier is the pitch you hear, and the beat rate is how many times a second it pulses. They are separate settings, and a track should say which is which.

What the research supports

Binaural beats have the most research. A meta-analysis of lab studies found a medium-sized effect on attention, memory, anxiety measures and pain perception.1 A systematic review of EEG studies found the usual explanation, that brain waves “follow” the beat, inconsistently supported.2 The meta-analysis covered binaural beats only; isochronic tones and tremolo have far less controlled research behind them. Specific “healing” pitches have only small pilot studies so far, or none yet.3 And the way most people actually listen, with tones under music they enjoy and sometimes spoken affirmations, has not been studied as a whole. The details, with numbers, are in our explainer on the research.

A fair summary. Some lab studies suggest small-to-moderate effects, and the mechanism is still being worked out. Many people say certain tracks help them settle or focus: notice how one makes you feel and keep what works for you, and be wary of any track that promises a specific outcome.

Listening sensibly

Binaural beats need headphones; the other kinds work on speakers. Keep the volume comfortable. Don’t listen while driving, operating machinery or doing anything that needs your full attention. If you have epilepsy or a seizure disorder, or are pregnant, check with a doctor before listening. Be wary of any track that names a medical condition, promises a result, or gives a timeline.
