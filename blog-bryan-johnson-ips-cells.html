Research · Stem cells November 16, 2026 By Bill Seery

Bryan Johnson turned his blood into stem cells. Here’s what you can actually buy today.

This weekend, longevity influencer Bryan Johnson told his email list he had “reprogrammed my adult cells to age zero,” and that “soon, you will be able to as well.” It’s a big claim with a real science story behind it. Here’s what he did, what the technology can do in 2026, and what you can actually buy.

What he did

Johnson had blood drawn. Scientists isolated his cells and treated them with Yamanaka factors, a set of four proteins (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, c-Myc) that wipe out a cell’s adult identity and return it to an embryo-like state. The result is induced pluripotent stem cells (iPS cells), which can in principle be grown into nearly any cell type: neurons, heart muscle, retina, kidney or stomach lining.

It isn’t a clone, and it isn’t new. Shinya Yamanaka shared the 2012 Nobel Prize for this discovery. Johnson’s interest is personal: in July he disclosed autoimmune gastritis, a condition in which the immune system attacks the stomach lining. He hasn’t named the lab that did the work or released any data.

One distinction matters a lot. The “age zero” reset happened to cells in a dish, not to Johnson. His body is the same age it was last week.

What iPS cells are doing in medicine right now

  • Japan approved the world’s first iPS cell products in March 2026. One is Amchepry, from Sumitomo Pharma, for Parkinson’s disease. The other is ReHeart, from Cuorips, for severe heart failure. Both approvals are conditional and were based on small trials. Amchepry’s key study treated seven patients, and four improved. The first commercial transplant took place at Kyoto University Hospital in October.
  • Both use donor cells, so patients need immune-suppressing drugs. Johnson’s pitch is the other model: your own cells, so no rejection. Aspen Neuroscience is testing exactly that for Parkinson’s, with encouraging early results in a small group of patients, and it plans a late-stage trial.
  • In the U.S., no iPS cell therapy is FDA-approved yet.

What you can buy: banking, not treatment

Several companies will turn your blood into iPS cells and freeze them for possible future use:

  • I Peace (Palo Alto / Kyoto). Its founder came out of Yamanaka’s own lab. It launched personal iPS cell banking in the U.S. in 2025.
  • iPS Portal (Kyoto, linked to Kyoto University’s iPS cell research center). It launched in April 2026 at ¥10–20 million, roughly $65,000–$130,000, and is aiming for 20 customers in its first year.
  • REPROCELL (Japan) has offered “Personal iPS” since 2021.

What you’re buying is an option, not a therapy. No approved treatment uses a stored personal iPS cell line today. The bet is that therapies built from your own cells arrive in your lifetime, and that younger cells banked now are better raw material than older cells banked later.

The catches

  • Reprogramming isn’t risk-free. Cells can pick up mutations while they’re reprogrammed and multiplied, which is why autologous programs (ones using the patient’s own cells) invest heavily in genetic quality checks before anything is transplanted.
  • Growing replacement organs is still years away, and there’s no evidence yet that injecting “young” iPS-derived cells rejuvenates the body as a whole.
  • Don’t confuse this with in-body reprogramming. A separate field, partial reprogramming, tries to make cells younger without turning them into stem cells. Life Biosciences dosed the first human in its trial in June 2026, targeting eye disease. That one is about rejuvenating tissue in place. Johnson’s cells are raw material in a freezer.

Bottom line

Johnson didn’t reverse his own aging. He did put a real, Nobel-backed technology in front of millions of people. iPS cell therapies are arriving, and Japan is leading. For most people, the practical question for now isn’t “should I get this?” It’s “is a $65,000+ insurance policy on future medicine worth it to me?”
