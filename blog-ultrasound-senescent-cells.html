Can sound waves revive aging cells? Inside the “ultrasonic hot tub” research.

Most anti-aging research on senescent cells tries to kill them. These are the worn-out “zombie” cells that stop dividing, refuse to die, and leak inflammatory signals into the tissue around them. A team in Texas tried something different: waking them back up with sound.

The discovery

Researchers at the University of Texas Medical Branch in Galveston, led by the late cell biologist Michael Sheetz, were using ultrasound to kill cancer cells. When they aimed it at senescent cells instead, the cells didn’t die. They started moving and dividing again.

The work was published in Aging Cell in 2025. The settings were specific: low-frequency ultrasound at about 32 kHz, pulsed on and off, delivered through warm water for about 30 minutes. That’s far below the frequencies used for imaging. In the lab, treated senescent cells:

  • resumed dividing, with no sign the treatment was killing them
  • showed lower levels of the standard senescence markers
  • released fewer inflammatory signals
  • grew smaller, more mobile mitochondria

The proposed mechanism is mechanical. The pressure waves let calcium into the cell, which kicks off the cell’s cleanup process (autophagy) and turns down mTOR, a growth pathway already familiar from rapamycin research.

In old mice

The team treated mice that were already old, 22–25 months, well into old age for a mouse. Treated mice performed better on treadmill and grip tests and grew thicker, darker fur. Senescence markers in the kidney and pancreas fell. There was a lifespan signal, but it was statistically significant only when two treatment groups were pooled, not for either one on its own.

What’s happening now

  • A human trial is underway. UT Health San Antonio’s Barshop Institute moved the research “ultrasonic hot tub” there and is running an 8-week study in adults 70 and older: bath sessions three times a week, compared with a sham bath that has the ultrasound switched off. About 20 people are enrolling, and the estimated end date is December 2026.
  • XPRIZE took notice. The team won $250,000 in the first round of the $101 million XPRIZE Healthspan competition.
  • A company is forming. Mechanobiologics Inc., in which two of the study’s authors hold a financial interest, plans to sell ultrasound devices for this use. As of today it has announced no product, price or regulatory clearance.

Can you buy one?

No. Ultrasonic baths, spa gadgets and cleaners are sold online, but none of them is the research device or uses the tested settings, and none has data behind it. Ultrasound is safe and routine in many medical uses. That doesn’t mean a home gadget does what a calibrated lab device did in mice.

Why it matters

If the human data holds up, this could become one of the rare longevity interventions that is drug-free, non-invasive and passive: you sit in warm water. That’s a big if. The mouse work is from a single lab, has methodological loose ends and hasn’t been independently repeated. The San Antonio trial results are the next thing to watch.
