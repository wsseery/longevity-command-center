Resveratrol and the NIA mouse lifespan tests: null three times

Resveratrol is one of the best-known names in longevity. In the National Institute on Aging's mouse lifespan program it was tested three times and came up null each time. Here is what that result means, and what it does not.

Resveratrol was tested three times in the NIA mouse lifespan tests, and it came up null each time. That is the plain summary of the program's record on a compound that has attracted more attention than almost any other in aging research. This post explains how the program works, why a null result there carries weight, and how it compares with a compound that did extend lifespan.

What the NIA mouse lifespan tests are

The NIA Interventions Testing Program (ITP) tests compounds for their effect on lifespan in mice. As the compounds scoreboard on this site describes it, each compound is tested at three independent sites (Jackson Laboratory, the University of Michigan and UT Health San Antonio) in genetically heterogeneous mice. Running the same test at several sites in a varied mouse population is meant to make a positive or a null result harder to explain away as a quirk of one lab or one strain.

What “null” means here

Resveratrol was tested three times in the program, and none of those tests found an extension of lifespan. A null result is not the same as proof of harm, and it is not a verdict on every possible dose, schedule or species. It means that under the program's standardized conditions, the compound did not make the mice live longer. On the scoreboard, resveratrol carries the “no effect” icon for exactly that reason. The ITP's resveratrol work is reported in the program's published results, including Strong et al., 2013.

Mouse results, not human ones. Nothing in the ITP record tells you what resveratrol does in people. The scoreboard lists human trial status separately, and it is a different question with a different evidence base.

The contrast: rapamycin

The same program has produced positive results. Rapamycin extended lifespan by up to 23% in male mice and 26% in female mice, according to Miller et al., 2014. The comparison is useful because both compounds went through the same testing framework. The framework can return a clear positive, which makes a repeated null more informative rather than less.

Those rapamycin figures are also mouse data. They show what the program can detect in mice, not what any compound does for a person.

How to read this

  • Popularity is not evidence. A compound can be famous and still have a null record in the most standardized lifespan test available.
  • Check the test design. Multiple sites and a genetically varied population are what give an ITP result its weight.
  • Keep species separate. A mouse lifespan result, positive or null, is not a human result.
  • See every compound side by side. The compounds scoreboard lays out the ITP results next to human trial status. For how this fits into the wider field, see the post on drug repurposing and the hallmarks of aging, or return to the home page.
