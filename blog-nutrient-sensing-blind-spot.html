The gut-health plate has a blind spot

David Sinclair's gut-health episode tells you to eat in a way that lowers inflammation and supports nutrient sensing. It is good advice. It also assumes the foods on that list agree with you — and on 143 of the 440 foods we scored, the lists flatly disagree with each other.

On September 11, 2026, Lifespan with Dr. David Sinclair published “How to Eat for Gut Health & Longevity” (Season 2, Episode 8). In 31 minutes it walks through four ideas that have become the spine of how nutrition gets talked about in longevity: glycation, which the episode describes as the process by which sugar attaches to proteins and disrupts their function; the rise of inflammation with age; the way the gut microbiome shifts over a lifetime; and the decline in the body's ability to sense and respond to nutrients. The practical advice follows from that: cut the dietary drivers of inflammation and excess sugar, and support gut health and the nutrient-sensing pathways.

None of that is controversial, and this piece is not an argument against it. It is an argument about a group of people the advice quietly skips.

Nutrient sensing, in plain English

Your cells do not eat. They read. A set of molecular switches samples what is in the blood and decides whether the body is in a time of plenty or a time of scarcity, and then sets the cell's priorities to match — grow and store, or conserve and repair. The main ones are insulin and IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1), which report that sugar and protein have arrived; mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin), the growth switch; AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase), the low-fuel alarm; and the sirtuins, a family of enzymes tied to the repair side of that trade-off.

When that signalling drifts — when the switches stop reading the situation accurately — the field calls it deregulated nutrient sensing, and it is one of the hallmarks of aging. In the 2023 update to that framework, “Hallmarks of aging: an expanding universe,” two of the newly added hallmarks were chronic inflammation and dysbiosis, an out-of-balance gut microbiome. Diet, inflammation, the microbiome and nutrient sensing are not four separate topics that happen to share an episode. They are four faces of the same system.

The blind spot

Here is the problem. The foods most often recommended for that system are, for a subset of people, the foods that set off symptoms.

Take the best evidence there is for fermented food. In a 2021 Stanford trial published in Cell, 36 healthy adults spent 10 weeks on either a high-fibre diet or a fermented-food diet. In the fermented-food group, gut microbial diversity went up and the levels of 19 inflammatory proteins in blood went down, including interleukin 6. In the high-fibre group over that period, diversity held steady and no inflammatory markers fell. It is a genuinely striking result, and it is why yogurt, kefir, kimchi, kombucha and fermented vegetables show up on every gut-health list.

Those same foods are, by the mechanism of fermentation itself, among the highest-histamine foods there are. In the Crosscheck Kitchen dataset, sauerkraut and kimchi both score 3 out of 3 for histamine and essentially zero for lectins. Our own note on sauerkraut reads: “Lactic-acid fermentation makes this one of the highest-histamine foods there is.”

And it is not only the fermented shelf. Some of the most recommended plants in longevity nutrition land in the same awkward place:

Average those two columns into a single “wellness score” and every one of these foods lands in the harmless middle, which is exactly the wrong answer for the person who reacts to one of the two scales. That is why the tool refuses to average them.

Two mechanisms, two very different evidence bases

It would be dishonest to present histamine and lectins as equally well established. They are not, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

Histamine intolerance is the better-supported of the two. The working idea is that some people break histamine down slowly in the gut — the enzyme usually named is DAO (diamine oxidase) — so that a normal dietary load produces symptoms. The food scores in the tool come from the food-compatibility list published by SIGHI, the Swiss Interest Group Histamine Intolerance. But there is no single validated test that settles the question for an individual: in practice diagnosis still rests on a supervised elimination and a careful reintroduction, which is slow, and which is why people guess instead.

Lectins are the weaker case, and the tool says so on every surface that shows a lectin score. What is solidly true is narrow: raw and undercooked kidney beans contain phytohaemagglutinin and cause real, acute poisoning, and proper soaking and boiling destroys it. The much broader popular claim — that ordinary, properly cooked lectin-containing plants drive chronic inflammation in healthy people — is not established. It also runs against a substantial body of observational evidence pointing the other way: in the Food Habits in Later Life study of adults over 70 across four countries, every 20 g/day increase in legume intake was associated with an 8% lower risk of death, and legumes were the one food group that held up across every ethnic group in the analysis.

So why score lectins at all? Because people are already avoiding these foods, usually on the strength of a popular book and a blog list, and usually with no idea which items are actually cookable back to baseline. A scored list that says “this one is fixable in a pressure cooker, this one is not, and by the way the evidence here is the weakest of the three scales” is more useful than either a purity list or a flat dismissal.

What to do with this

The episode's advice stands. If the gut-health plate works for you, eat it. This is for the people it does not work for — the ones who added sauerkraut and spinach and felt worse, concluded they had failed at healthy eating, and quietly went back to what they were eating before.

  • Separate the scales before you edit your diet. “I react to fermented food” and “I react to plants” are different claims with different fixes, and a single blended score hides which one you are dealing with.
  • Check preparation before elimination. In the dataset, 43 foods carry a lectin score that preparation meaningfully lowers — peeling and deseeding a tomato, pressure-cooking peas. Dropping a food outright is the bigger cost, and it is often unnecessary.
  • Keep a plain symptom log. What you ate, when, and how you felt some hours later. It is unglamorous and it is the only input that is actually about you rather than about a population.
  • Take that log to a clinician — a physician or a registered dietitian — before removing whole food groups, especially if you are restricting the legumes and vegetables that the longevity literature spends most of its time recommending.

The deeper point is the one the episode makes well and the internet keeps flattening: aging nutrition is about signals, not about a list of virtuous foods. A food that lowers inflammatory signalling in most people and raises it in you is not a good food or a bad food. It is information about your system — and it is worth more than a verdict.
