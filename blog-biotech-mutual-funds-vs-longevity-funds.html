Biotech mutual funds vs longevity funds: what the labels actually mean

“Biotech fund” names a kind of holding. “Longevity fund” is usually a theme, and it can mean a public ETF anyone can buy or a private venture fund most people cannot. Here is how the structures differ, in plain terms, before any question of what to own.

People who follow aging research often end up asking a money question: is there a fund for this? Search for one and you meet two labels that sound alike. A biotech mutual fund is a well-defined product. A “longevity fund” is not a regulatory category at all; the name gets used for at least two very different things. This explainer sorts them out. It describes structures only. It is not a recommendation to buy or sell anything.

What a biotech mutual fund is

A mutual fund pools investors’ money into a portfolio, and a biotech mutual fund holds biotechnology companies. Fidelity Select Biotechnology Portfolio is one long-running example. The SEC’s investor education site explains the mechanics: investors buy and sell mutual fund shares “from/to the fund itself or through a broker or investment adviser, rather than from/to other investors,” and the purchase price is “the next calculated NAV” (net asset value), plus any fees. Shares are redeemable: you can sell them back to the fund at the next calculated NAV, minus any fees.

Biotech ETFs: the same idea, traded like a stock

An exchange-traded fund also pools a portfolio, but its shares trade on stock exchanges. In the SEC’s words, ETF investors can trade “at the market price whenever the market is open,” and that price “may or may not be the same as the NAV.” The broad biotech funds most people mean when they say “biotech index” are ETFs, such as the iShares Biotechnology ETF (IBB) and the State Street SPDR S&P Biotech ETF (XBI). Both are on our Finance tracker, which follows 11 aging- and biotech-related ETFs.

What “longevity fund” usually means

A public, aging-themed fund. Some ETFs build a portfolio around the aging population rather than around aging biology. On our tracker that is the Global X Aging Population ETF (AGNG). Its largest holdings, as the tracker lists them, are large drug makers such as Eli Lilly, AbbVie and Amgen, companies whose pipelines go well beyond aging. Amgen and Regeneron also sit among IBB’s largest holdings, so an aging-themed fund and a broad biotech fund can overlap more than their names suggest.

A private venture fund focused on aging biology. This is what the longevity field itself usually means by a longevity fund: venture capital that backs early-stage companies working on the biology of aging. Our tracker lists several, including Apollo Health Ventures (founded 2017), the Methuselah Fund (2017) and the Longevity Vision Fund (2019). These are private funds, not products you can buy in a brokerage account, and private offerings are generally limited to accredited investors.

Who counts as an accredited investor. Under the SEC’s definition, an individual qualifies with earned income over $200,000 (or $300,000 together with a spouse or spousal equivalent) in each of the prior two years and a reasonable expectation of the same this year; or a net worth over $1 million, alone or with a spouse or spousal equivalent, not counting the primary residence; or by holding a Series 7, 65 or 82 license in good standing.

Costs are visible on the public side

Public funds publish an expense ratio, the yearly cost as a share of assets. As our tracker listed them on November 24, 2026: XBI 0.35%, IBB 0.44%, AGNG 0.50% and the ARK Genomic Revolution ETF (ARKG) 0.75%. A fund’s own prospectus is the authoritative figure, and it can change. Private venture funds set their fees in their offering documents, which are not public in the same way.

The short version

  • “Biotech” describes holdings; “longevity” usually describes a theme. Look past the name to what the fund actually holds.
  • Mutual fund or ETF is about mechanics: priced at the next NAV and redeemed with the fund, or traded on an exchange at a market price.
  • Most of the money aimed squarely at aging biology is private, in venture funds that are generally open only to accredited investors.
